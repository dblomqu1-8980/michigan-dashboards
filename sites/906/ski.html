Why The West Gets More

Lake-effect snow, and how to read it

The single most useful thing to understand about UP skiing is that snowfall is wildly uneven across the peninsula, and it is uneven for a reason you can predict.

❄️ How the bands form
Cold arctic air crosses the open, relatively warm water of Lake Superior, picks up moisture and heat, then dumps it as snow when it hits land and is forced up over higher terrain. The longer the air travels over open water, the more it carries.
🧭 Why the west wins
Gogebic and Ontonagon counties sit downwind of the widest stretch of Lake Superior and hold the highest ground in Michigan. Ironwood, Bessemer and Wakefield can measure a storm in feet while the eastern UP measures the same system in inches.
🌊 The ice-cover catch
Lake-effect needs open water. In a hard winter the lake ices over and the machine shuts off — late-season snow then depends on synoptic storms instead. Check the Lake Superior panel on the main dashboard for current water temperature.
Know Before You Go

Passes, timing and the drive

🎟️ Trail passes
Private Nordic centers such as ABR charge a daily trail fee. Valley Spur asks for a pass or donation that funds grooming. State pathways and state park trails need a Michigan Recreation Passport on the vehicle rather than a per-skier fee. Club systems such as Swedetown and Wolverine Nordic run on memberships and donations.
🗓️ When to come
Most downhill areas open late November to mid-December and run through late March. Mount Bohemia makes no snow at all — it opens when nature delivers and often skis into mid-April. January and February are the reliable months everywhere.
🚗 Getting there
The same lake-effect that fills the hills closes the roads. Check live road conditions before a western-UP drive, and remember Gogebic, Iron, Dickinson and Menominee counties are on Central Time — an hour behind the rest of the peninsula.

Frequently Asked

UP skiing questions

When does ski season start in the Upper Peninsula?
Most UP downhill areas open between late November and mid-December and run through late March. Mount Bohemia is the outlier — it makes no snow at all, so it opens only when nature delivers, typically in December, and it often skis into mid-April. Groomed Nordic systems need a settled base and generally start grooming in December.
Which UP ski area gets the most snow?
The western snowbelt — Gogebic, Ontonagon and Keweenaw counties. Mount Bohemia on the Keweenaw averages roughly 270 inches a season and has recorded seasons over 300 inches, the deepest natural snowfall in the Midwest. Ironwood, Bessemer and Wakefield are close behind. See the lake-effect explainer for why.
Where can I cross-country ski in the UP?
The largest groomed systems are ABR Trails in Ironwood (about 60 km groomed daily), Valley Spur near Munising (about 45 km in the Hiawatha National Forest), Swedetown Trails in Calumet (about 35 km, 5 km lit for night skiing) and Blueberry Ridge Pathway near Marquette (about 21 km with a 2.7 km lit loop). All are groomed for both skate and classic. The full list is in the Nordic section.
Do I need a trail pass to cross-country ski in the UP?
It depends who grooms it. Private centers such as ABR charge a daily trail fee. Valley Spur asks for a trail pass or donation that pays for grooming. State pathways and state park trails require a Michigan Recreation Passport on your vehicle rather than a per-skier fee. Club-groomed systems such as Swedetown and Wolverine Nordic run on memberships and donations — pay them, they are why the trails are groomed.
What is lake-effect snow and why does the western UP get so much?
Cold arctic air crosses the open, relatively warm water of Lake Superior, picks up moisture and heat, and dumps it as snow when it hits land and rises over higher terrain. The western UP sits downwind of the widest stretch of the lake and holds the highest ground in Michigan, so it collects the heaviest bands. It also means the machine can switch off: lake-effect needs open water, so a hard freeze on Superior cuts it back.
